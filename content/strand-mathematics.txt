STRAND 4: QUADRATIC EQUATIONS AND EXPRESSIONS
================================================================================

DESIGN PAGE 12 — STRAND OVERVIEW
Quadratic expressions and equations form the foundation for later work in
functions, coordinate geometry, and rates of change. Learners at Grade 10 are
expected to move from linear to quadratic relationships and to interpret what
solutions mean in real-life situations.
By the end of this strand, the learner should be able to:
  a) expand and factorise quadratic expressions;
  b) solve quadratic equations by factorisation, completing the square, and the
     quadratic formula;
  c) form and solve quadratic equations from worded problems;
  d) interpret the solutions of a quadratic equation graphically.
Time allocation: 40-minute lessons. Suggested total for the strand: 8 lessons.
Key inquiry questions: "When is a quadratic equation the right model for a
problem?", "What does it mean when a quadratic equation has no real solutions?"

DESIGN PAGE 13 — LESSON 1: EXPANDING AND FACTORISING QUADRATIC EXPRESSIONS
Sub-strand: Algebra.
Specific outcomes: by the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to
expand products of two linear expressions such as (x + 3)(x - 2), identify a
quadratic expression ax^2 + bx + c, and factorise simple quadratics with
integer roots.
Suggested learning experiences: learners expand brackets using the distributive
law; a double-sided number-line or area model (rectangle with side lengths
(x + a) and (x + b)) is recommended to make factorisation visual.
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, graph paper, printed area cards.
Assessment: oral questions and a quick-check exercise of 5 factorisation items.
Misconception to watch: learners often expand (x + a)(x + b) and forget the
middle term, writing x^2 + ab. Emphasise the four products (FOIL) and the area
model.

DESIGN PAGE 14 — LESSON 2: SOLVING BY FACTORISATION
Prerequisite: Lesson 1 (factorising quadratic expressions).
Specific outcomes: solve quadratic equations of the form ax^2 + bx + c = 0 with
a = 1 by factorisation and state the solution set.
Key point: the zero-product property — if p · q = 0 then p = 0 or q = 0.
Suggested learning experiences: learners solve x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0 by writing
(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0 and reading off x = 2 or x = 3; link each root to where the
corresponding graph crosses the x-axis.
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, graph paper.
Assessment: quick-check items such as x^2 - 9 = 0 and x^2 - 4x = 0.
Misconception to watch: learners divide both sides by x when x is a factor
(x^2 - 4x = 0), losing the root x = 0. Remind them never to divide by a variable
that could be zero; factorise instead.

DESIGN PAGE 15 — LESSON 3: COMPLETING THE SQUARE
Prerequisite: Lessons 1-2; squaring and square roots.
Specific outcomes: solve quadratic equations by completing the square, including
equations with irrational solutions, and express answers in surd form where
appropriate.
Suggested learning experiences: start from (x + p)^2 = x^2 + 2px + p^2; show
that x^2 + 6x + 2 = 0 becomes (x + 3)^2 = 7, hence x = -3 +- sqrt(7).
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, algebra tiles where available.
Assessment: solve x^2 + 4x - 1 = 0 by completing the square.
Misconception to watch: learners forget to balance the equation when adding the
constant term (p^2) to both sides, or misplace the sign of the constant in the
final bracket.

DESIGN PAGE 16 — LESSON 4: THE QUADRATIC FORMULA
Prerequisite: completing the square.
Specific outcomes: derive and use the quadratic formula x = (-b +- sqrt(b^2 - 4ac)) / 2a
for a quadratic equation ax^2 + bx + c = 0, and use the discriminant b^2 - 4ac
to describe the nature of the roots (two real, one repeated, or no real roots).
Suggested learning experiences: learners apply the formula to equations that do
not factorise easily, e.g. 2x^2 - 3x - 5 = 0; discuss what the discriminant
tells them before they compute the roots.
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, scientific calculators (optional).
Assessment: classify 3 equations by discriminant, then solve 2 by formula.
Misconception to watch: learners mis-substitute the sign of b, especially when
b is negative, and forget that the formula applies only when the equation is in
standard form ax^2 + bx + c = 0 with all terms on one side.

DESIGN PAGE 17 — LESSON 5: WORDED PROBLEMS AND APPLICATIONS
Prerequisite: Lessons 1-4.
Specific outcomes: form a quadratic equation from a worded problem, solve it,
and interpret both the mathematical and the real-life meaning of the solutions.
Suggested learning experiences: use the classic product-and-sum problem (two
numbers whose product is 12 and sum is 7) and an area problem: a rectangular
plot whose length is 3 m more than its width has area 40 m^2 — find the
dimensions. Reject solutions that are not sensible in the context (a negative
length).
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, measuring tape for a practical task.
Assessment: one worded problem solved and interpreted in writing.
Misconception to watch: learners accept negative or fractional answers without
checking them against the story of the problem.

DESIGN PAGE 18 — LESSON 6: GRAPHICAL SOLUTION AND REVIEW
Prerequisite: Lessons 1-5.
Specific outcomes: sketch the graph of y = ax^2 + bx + c, read the x-intercepts
as solutions of ax^2 + bx + c = 0, and connect the discriminant to the number of
x-intercepts.
Suggested learning experiences: plot y = x^2 - 5x + 6 and confirm the roots read
from the graph match factorisation; discuss what a graph that never touches the
x-axis means for the equation.
Suggested resources: graph paper, ruler, chalkboard.
Assessment: strand quick check covering one item from each of Lessons 1-6.
Misconception to watch: learners confuse the turning point of the graph with the
roots of the equation. The roots are where the curve crosses the x-axis.

================================================================================
CROSS-CUTTING NOTES (DESIGN PAGE 19)
================================================================================
Differentiation: provide extra practice on factorisation for learners who need
it and extension tasks (deriving the formula) for faster learners.
Inclusive education: use concrete area models and allow pair work.
Linkages: quadratic functions are revisited in Grade 11 coordinate geometry and
in the physics topic of projectile motion.
