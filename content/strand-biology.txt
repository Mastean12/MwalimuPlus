================================================================================
STRAND 2: CELLULAR RESPIRATION
================================================================================

DESIGN PAGE 20 — STRAND OVERVIEW
Cellular respiration is the process by which cells release energy from glucose.
Grade 10 learners build on the Grade 9 introduction to cells and energy, and
move to the chemical details of respiration and its importance to living
organisms.
By the end of this strand, the learner should be able to:
  a) state the word and symbol equation for aerobic respiration;
  b) describe the role of mitochondria in energy release;
  c) compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration in humans and in
     micro-organisms;
  d) relate respiration rate to exercise and explain the oxygen debt.
Time allocation: 40-minute lessons. Suggested total for the strand: 6 lessons.
Key inquiry question: "Where does the energy in our food actually come from?"

DESIGN PAGE 21 — LESSON 1: RELEASING ENERGY FROM FOOD
Sub-strand: Life processes.
Specific outcomes: state that glucose is the main respiratory substrate, write
the word equation for aerobic respiration (glucose + oxygen -> carbon dioxide +
water + energy), and identify where respiration happens in the cell
(mitochondria).
Suggested learning experiences: learners compare burning food (demonstration by
the teacher) with respiration, and label a diagram of a cell showing
mitochondria.
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, chart of the cell, small food samples.
Assessment: oral questions and a labelling exercise.
Misconception to watch: learners think respiration is the same as breathing.
Distinguish breathing (gas exchange in the lungs) from respiration (energy
release in cells).

DESIGN PAGE 22 — LESSON 2: AEROBIC RESPIRATION
Prerequisite: Lesson 1.
Specific outcomes: write the balanced symbol equation for aerobic respiration
C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (about 2 900 kJ per mole of glucose), and
explain the role of mitochondria as the site of aerobic respiration.
Suggested learning experiences: learners use molecular models or cards to
balance the equation and count atoms on both sides.
Suggested resources: chalkboard, chalk, atom cards or models.
Assessment: balance the equation and explain the role of mitochondria in one
short paragraph.
Misconception to watch: learners omit the energy term or mis-balance the carbon
dioxide. Balancing by atom-counting on both sides is the recommended check.

DESIGN PAGE 23 — LESSON 3: ANAEROBIC RESPIRATION
Prerequisite: Lesson 2.
Specific outcomes: describe anaerobic respiration in humans (glucose -> lactic
acid + energy, no oxygen) and in yeast (glucose -> ethanol + carbon dioxide +
energy), and state where each occurs.
Suggested learning experiences: the classic experiment with yeast in warm
sugared water producing bubbles of carbon dioxide; relate human anaerobic
respiration to vigorous exercise.
Suggested resources: yeast, sugar, warm water, test tubes, balloon.
Assessment: table comparing aerobic and anaerobic respiration (site, reactants,
products, energy yield).
Misconception to watch: learners believe anaerobic respiration produces no
energy at all. Clarify that it releases far less energy per glucose molecule,
not zero.

DESIGN PAGE 24 — LESSON 4: RESPIRATION AND EXERCISE
Prerequisite: Lesson 3.
Specific outcomes: explain why breathing rate and heart rate increase during
exercise, define oxygen debt as the extra oxygen needed after exercise to break
down lactic acid, and describe recovery.
Suggested learning experiences: learners measure their own pulse and breathing
rate before, during, and after light exercise; results are graphed and
discussed.
Suggested resources: stopwatch, graph paper, chalkboard.
Assessment: interpret a simple graph of breathing rate during and after
exercise.
Misconception to watch: learners think lactic acid is removed immediately.
Explain that the oxygen debt is repaid during recovery as lactic acid is broken
down (and some is oxidised) after exercise stops.

CROSS-CUTTING NOTES (DESIGN PAGE 25)

Differentiation: provide sentence starters for the word equation and extension
on the energy released per gram of substrate.
Inclusive education: allow demonstration observations and pair discussion.
Linkages: respiration links to Grade 11 nutrition and to the chemistry of
carbon compounds.
